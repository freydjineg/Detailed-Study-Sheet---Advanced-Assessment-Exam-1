🩺 Module 1 — Health Assessment, Communication & Fundamentals

🗣️The big idea: Before you touch a patient, you talk to them. Before you make a clinical decision, you observe and listen. Module 1 is about how nurses communicate, how they think, and how they support patients at the most vulnerable moments of their lives — including death. Everything in this module feeds into Tanner's Clinical Judgment Model.

💬 Types of Communication

Verbal Communication

Vocal Paralanguage matters as much as words:
Tone · Pitch · Volume · Rate of speech

A nurse who says "I understand" in a flat, rushed tone communicates the opposite. The HOW you say it is often more powerful than WHAT you say.
  • Face-to-face, phone, documentation
  • Clarity, conciseness, and jargon-free language
  • Written communication (charting) is legal documentation — be objective and specific

Nonverbal Communication

Up to 80% of communication is nonverbal.
Body language · Facial expressions · Affect · Posture · Eye contact · Touch · Dress and grooming · Use of time
  • Patients read your face before they hear your words
  • Entering a room calmly vs. hurriedly sends a message
  • Touch: therapeutic but culturally sensitive — always explain before touching

🧰 Therapeutic Communication Techniques

👂Therapeutic communication is purposeful — every technique has a reason. The goal is always to help the patient feel heard, understood, and safe enough to share what you need to know clinically.

✅ Therapeutic Techniques

  • Validating — Restate words as you heard them: "So what you're saying is..." Confirms you heard correctly.
  • Reflecting — Repeat the feeling or implied emotion: "It sounds like you're frightened about the results."
  • Clarifying — Seek understanding: "Can you help me understand what you mean by that?"
  • Directing/Focusing — Ask for elaboration: "Tell me more about that chest pain."
  • Sequencing — Understand the timeline: "Walk me through exactly what happened, step by step."
  • General Leads — Keep them talking: "Go on..." / "Tell me more..." / "And then?"
  • Therapeutic Silence — Sit quietly and be present. Gives the patient time to process. One of the most powerful tools.
  • Open-ended questions — "How have you been feeling since the diagnosis?" Cannot be answered with yes/no.

❌ Non-Therapeutic Barriers

  • False reassurance: "You'll be fine!" — You can't promise that. Closes the conversation and invalidates their fear.
  • Leading questions: "You don't have allergies, do you?" — Biases toward "no." Always neutral phrasing.
  • False empathy: "I know exactly how you feel." — You don't. Say: "That sounds incredibly difficult."
  • Giving advice prematurely — Before the patient feels heard, advice feels dismissive.
  • Changing the subject — Signals the topic is uncomfortable and the patient stops sharing.
  • Asking "why" — "Why didn't you take your medications?" sounds judgmental. Say: "Help me understand what made it difficult."

Proxemics — Personal Space Zones (Know for Exam)

🤝 Intimate Zone — <18 inches
Physical assessment, IV insertion, wound care, bathing. Patient must consent to this space.
👋 Personal Zone — 18 in to 4 ft
Health interviews, patient teaching, therapeutic conversations. This is where most nurse-patient interaction happens.
🗣️ Social Zone — 4 to 12 ft
General conversation, initial greetings, chart review at bedside. Still interactive but less personal.
📢 Public Zone — >12 ft
Lectures, public speaking, group education sessions. NEVER appropriate for sensitive health discussions.

🛡️ Patient Advocacy

⚖️Advocacy means the patient's right to make informed decisions about their own body. The nurse's role is to ensure the patient has the information they need — not to make the decision for them.
❌ Not Advocacy:
"Surgery is your only choice. You need this operation."
"If you don't have surgery, you may not see your grandchildren."
— These are coercive and remove patient autonomy.
✅ Advocacy:
"Whether or not you have the surgery is your choice. It is your body."
"Let me make sure you have all the information you need to make this decision."
— This is person-centered care.

👥 Communication with Special Populations

Key principle for ALL special populations: Adapt your technique to meet the patient where they are. Never assume. Always assess first.
  • Children: Talk at eye level · Be gentle and calm · Use simple age-appropriate language · Use structured play · Respond to nonverbal cues · Parents can support but don't always translate — speak TO the child
  • Critically Ill/Unconscious: Assume they can hear you (hearing is the last sense lost) · Talk about what you are doing · Clear, direct, simple explanations · Provide day/time cues · Nonverbal touch is meaningful · Call bell within reach
  • Older Adults: Never assume hearing or vision impairment — assess first · Be gentle entering room · Use simple language · Restatement and clarification · Avoid elderspeak (high-pitched, patronizing tone = ageism)
  • Deaf/Hard of Hearing: Face the patient directly · Do NOT chew gum or cover mouth · Use simple sentences at normal pace · Write ideas if needed · Ensure hearing aids are in and working · Use sign language if able
  • Visually Impaired: Announce your presence and name · Speak in normal tone · Explain before touching · Indicate when conversation ends and when leaving · Orient to room sounds and layout · Ensure glasses/contacts in place
  • Physical Barrier (Laryngectomy/ET tube): Select simple communication tools (eye blinks, writing pad, picture board, hand signals) · Ensure the whole team knows the method · Be patient with time needed
  • Cognitively Impaired: Eye contact to hold attention · Quiet environment · Simple concrete language · Break instructions into small steps · Avoid open-ended questions — offer limited choices · Give 2 minutes to respond before repeating
  • Non-English Speaking: Use a professional interpreter — not family members for sensitive information (confidentiality and accuracy concerns) · Speak simply and at normal volume · Pantomime as appropriate · Be aware that many nonverbal cues are universal
🔴 Cultural Sensitivity: People from different cultures may have different norms around eye contact, touch, personal space, gender of provider, and disclosure of diagnosis. The goal is to treat everyone as an INDIVIDUAL — not to apply stereotypes. Ask, don't assume.

📋 SBAR — Structured Communication Tool

📞SBAR is the standardized format for communicating critical patient information to providers, during handoffs, and within the interprofessional team. It ensures nothing important gets left out.
S — Situation
"I'm calling about Mr. James in Room 412. He is having new onset chest pain rated 8/10, started 20 minutes ago."
B — Background
"He is a 68-year-old male admitted 2 days ago for chest pain workup. PMH: hypertension, diabetes. Current vitals: BP 162/94, HR 96."
A — Assessment
"His 12-lead ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF. Troponin trending upward from 0.04 to 0.38 over 2 hours. I believe this is an inferior STEMI."
R — Recommendation
"I recommend activating the cardiac catheterization lab immediately. He needs urgent intervention. Can you come to the floor now?"
💡 SBAR is also used for nursing handoff (shift report), rapid response calls, escalation of deteriorating patients, and interprofessional team huddles.

🧠 Tanner's Model of Clinical Judgment

🕵️Think of Tanner's model as the nurse's detective process. You don't just react to what you see — you gather data (Noticing), figure out what it means (Interpreting), decide what to do (Responding), and then learn from what happened (Reflecting). Mr. James from the lecture is the case example.
1. NOTICING — "What do I see?"
Open-ended questions + active observation. Gather the full clinical picture including SDOH (social determinants of health).

Mr. James: Nurse Susan notices diaphoresis, SpO2 92%, BP 168/96, HR 102, occupational chemical exposure, financial barriers to care.
2. INTERPRETING — "What does it mean?"
Integrate objective + subjective data. Use teach-back and empathetic listening to validate understanding.

Mr. James: Possible poorly controlled HTN + occupational lung disease + early cardiac involvement + SDOH barriers = complex multi-system risk.
3. RESPONDING — "What will I do?"
Advocacy + collaborative goal-setting. The nurse doesn't just treat the presenting symptom — they address the whole person.

Mr. James: Orders chest X-ray, ECG, spirometry. Refers to social work. Provides smoking cessation resources. Tailors education to low-cost dietary options.
4. REFLECTING — "What did I learn?"
Debrief + self-reflection. Identify what worked and what could be improved. Bring insights to the team.

Mr. James: Susan reflects she would have missed chronic contributors if she'd focused only on immediate symptoms. Brings case to team huddle. Considers SDOH screening tools.
Communication Techniques Aligned with Each Step:
Noticing → Open-ended questions, active observation
Interpreting → Teach-back, empathetic listening
Responding → Advocacy, collaborative goal-setting
Reflecting → Debriefing, self-reflection
SDOH in Clinical Judgment: Social determinants of health (income, housing, food security, occupation, education, access to care) are clinical data. If you don't assess them, you will miss the root cause of the patient's problem. Responding to SDOH requires interprofessional collaboration — social work, case management, community resources.

🕊️ Grief, Loss & End of Life

Key Definitions

  • Grief = Internal emotional reaction to loss (invisible — felt inside)
  • Mourning = External expression of grief (visible — crying, rituals, ceremonies)
  • Bereavement = The state/time period of grieving after a loss
  • Dysfunctional grief = Abnormal or distorted grief — either unresolved (extends over a lengthy period, difficulty expressing feelings) or inhibited (suppressed, may manifest as somatic symptoms like abdominal pain or palpitations)

Palliative vs. Hospice Care

Palliative Care
Comprehensive symptom management at ANY stage of illness. Can occur alongside curative treatment. Available inpatient and outpatient. Addresses physical, spiritual, psychosocial needs. Interdisciplinary.
Hospice Care
Life expectancy ≤6 months. Patient accepts death is imminent. Curative treatment STOPS. Focus = quality of life for remaining time. Patient AND family are the unit of care. Includes bereavement support for family after death.

Kübler-Ross Five Stages (DABDA)

  • Denial: "They made a mistake with my records. This can't be right." — Patient needs time to process. Do NOT force reality. Be present.
  • Anger: "Why me? I did everything right. This isn't fair!" — Allow expression. Do NOT take it personally. Validate the emotion: "Your anger makes complete sense."
  • Bargaining: "If I can just make it to my daughter's wedding, I'll be ready." — Classic conditional acceptance. Support these wishes when possible. They help the patient move forward.
  • Depression: Withdrawal, crying, not talking much. "I won't see my grandchildren grow up." — This is NORMAL anticipatory grief. Do NOT force cheerfulness. Therapeutic silence and presence are the interventions.
  • Acceptance: "I've made my arrangements. I'm at peace." — Honor this peace. Be present. Not all patients reach this stage. Stages are NOT linear — patients cycle back and forth.
⚠️ Critical Point: Stages are descriptive, NOT prescriptive. Patients don't move through them in order, may skip stages, may be in multiple stages simultaneously, or may never reach acceptance. Meet the patient where they are — not where you think they should be.

Clinical Signs of Impending Death

Active Dying — Cardinal Signs (tell the family):
🫁 Cheyne-Stokes respirations (rhythmic waxing/waning, periods of apnea)
🦵 Mottling + cyanosis of extremities and dependent areas
🥶 Extremities cool to touch (vascular shutdown)
😶 Unresponsive to verbal or gentle touch
🔊 "Death rattle" — secretions pooling (not painful — reassure the family)
Other Signs of Impending Death:
Difficulty talking or swallowing
Nausea, flatus, abdominal distention
Urinary/bowel incontinence or constipation
Loss of movement, sensation, reflexes
Decreasing body temperature
Weak, slow, or irregular pulse
Decreasing blood pressure
Restlessness or agitation

End-of-Life Nursing Responsibilities

  • Therapeutic presence — Being physically present without needing to fix or explain is often the most powerful nursing intervention at end of life
  • Family communication — "These changes tell us that [name]'s body is naturally shutting down. What you see is expected and they are not in pain."
  • Postmortem care — Prepare body in anatomic position, replace dressings, remove tubes (unless autopsy), ID tags, follow communicable disease laws. Care of family: listen, offer solace, arrange viewing, provide private space for grieving.
  • Principle of Double Effect — Opioids for palliative comfort: intent = comfort, not death. Research shows adequate opioid dosing does NOT shorten life and may extend it by reducing physiologic stress.
  • Self-care for nurses — Death and dying takes a toll. Debrief with colleagues, use peer support, recognize your own grief responses are normal and valid.

❤️ Module 2 — Cardiac, Respiratory & Neurological Assessment

🔌Think of the heart like a house with electricity. The electrical system (wires) tells the muscle (walls) when to squeeze. When the wires work right = normal sinus rhythm. When the wires break or short-circuit = arrhythmia. Your job as a nurse is to read the ECG and figure out which wire is broken!

⚡ ECG Basics

Normal Intervals

  • P wave = atria depolarize (squeeze)
  • PR interval = SA node → AV node (0.12–0.20 sec / 3–5 small boxes)
  • QRS = ventricles depolarize (<0.12 sec / <3 boxes)
  • ST segment = isoelectric (flat = normal, elevated = STEMI!)
  • T wave = ventricles repolarize (recover)
  • QT interval = whole ventricular cycle (<0.44 sec)
  • U wave = prominent = HYPOKALEMIA!

Rate Calculation

300-Method (regular rhythms):
Count large boxes between R waves.
300 ÷ boxes = rate

4 boxes = 75 bpm ✓
5 boxes = 60 bpm ✓
3 boxes = 100 bpm ✓

Pacemaker Hierarchy

  • SA Node = 60–100 bpm (boss)
  • AV Node = 40–60 bpm (backup)
  • Purkinje = 20–40 bpm (last resort)

🫀 Rhythm Recognition

🕵️For EVERY rhythm ask: (1) Is there a P wave? (2) Is the rhythm regular? (3) What's the rate? (4) Is the QRS narrow or wide?
  • Normal Sinus Rhythm (NSR): Rate 60–100, regular, P before every QRS, PR 0.12–0.20, narrow QRS. ✅ All good.
  • Sinus Bradycardia: Same as NSR but rate <60. Treat ONLY if symptomatic → atropine 0.5mg IV.
  • Sinus Tachycardia: Same as NSR but rate >100. Treat the CAUSE (fever, pain, dehydration). Don't reach for rate meds first.
  • AFib: NO P waves, irregularly irregular, fibrillatory baseline, narrow QRS. Stroke risk 5×! Stable → rate control + anticoag. Unstable → synchronized cardioversion.
  • Atrial Flutter: Sawtooth waves 250–350 bpm, usually regular (2:1, 4:1 block).
  • SVT: Fast (150–250), regular, narrow, P waves hidden in T waves. Stable → vagal → adenosine. Unstable → synchronized cardioversion.
  • VTach: Wide QRS, regular, 100–250 bpm, no P waves. With pulse + stable → amiodarone. Unstable → synchronized cardioversion. Pulseless → defibrillate!
  • VFib: Totally chaotic, no organized waveforms. PULSELESS. SHOCKABLE → CPR + defib NOW.
  • Asystole: Flat line. NON-SHOCKABLE. Confirm in 2 leads → CPR + epi only.
  • PEA: Organized rhythm on monitor, NO pulse. NON-SHOCKABLE → CPR + epi + treat Hs & Ts.
🚨 Shockable vs Non-Shockable:
SHOCK: VFib ✅ | Pulseless VT ✅
DO NOT SHOCK: Asystole ❌ | PEA ❌

🏥 Heart Blocks

🚦Heart blocks = the electrical signal gets delayed or stopped on its way from the top of the heart to the bottom. First degree = slow but still gets through. Second degree = sometimes doesn't get through. Third degree = completely blocked.
  • 1st Degree: PR >0.20 sec, constant, every P conducts. NOT a true block — just delayed. No treatment needed if asymptomatic.
  • 2nd Degree Type I (Wenckebach): PR gets longer, longer, longer… then QRS drops, then resets. AV node level. Usually benign.
  • 2nd Degree Type II (Mobitz II): Constant PR, then SUDDEN dropped QRS with no warning. Infranodal. Dangerous → often needs pacemaker.
  • 3rd Degree (Complete): P waves and QRS are totally independent — no communication at all. Ventricular rate 20–45 bpm. Emergency → pacemaker.

🧠 Neurological Assessment

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

  • Eye = 1–4 | Verbal = 1–5 | Motor = 1–6
  • Total: 3–15. GCS ≤8 = severe (protect airway)
  • GCS 15 = fully alert and oriented
  • Motor is most prognostically significant

Posturing

  • Decorticate = arms FLEX toward core. Cortical level. Less severe.
  • Decerebrate = ALL EXTREMITIES extend rigid. Brainstem level. More severe.

ICP — Early vs Late Signs

EARLY (act NOW):
Restlessness + confusion
Unilateral extremity weakness
Worsening headache
New pupil changes
LATE (herniation happening):
Cushing's Triad: HTN + bradycardia + irregular respirations
Fixed dilated pupils
Decerebrate posturing
Widening pulse pressure

🫁 ABG Interpretation & COPD

ABG Normal Values

  • pH: 7.35–7.45
  • PaCO2: 35–45 (respiratory)
  • HCO3: 22–26 (metabolic)
  • PaO2: 80–100
ROME Method:
Respiratory = Opposite (pH↑ CO2↓ or pH↓ CO2↑)
Metabolic = Equal (pH↑ HCO3↑ or pH↓ HCO3↓)

COPD O2 Rule

⚠️ DANGER: COPD patients with chronic CO2 retention use HYPOXIA as their drive to breathe (not CO2).

Target SpO2: 88–92%
High-flow O2 → removes hypoxic drive → hypoventilation → CO2 narcosis

Best device: Venturi mask (most precise)

💓 Heart Sounds

  • S1 ("lub") = AV valves close (start of systole). Loudest at APEX (5th ICS MCL)
  • S2 ("dub") = Semilunar valves close (end of systole). Loudest at BASE (2nd ICS)
  • S3 (ventricular gallop) = early diastole. Volume overload → HEART FAILURE. Pathological in adults >40
  • S4 (atrial gallop) = late diastole. Stiff ventricle → HYPERTROPHY. ALWAYS pathological
  • Murmurs = turbulent blood flow. Systolic murmur at 2nd ICS RSB + symptoms = aortic stenosis until proven otherwise
Landmarks (All People Enjoy Time Magazine):
Aortic = 2ICS RSB | Pulmonic = 2ICS LSB | Erb's = 3ICS LSB (S1=S2) | Tricuspid = 4ICS LSB | Mitral = 5ICS MCL

👁️ Cranial Nerve Quick Review

🧠You have 12 cranial nerves. Think of them like 12 different remote controls — each one controls something different in your head and neck. You test them one at a time!
  • CN I — Olfactory: Smell the gum wrapper 👃 (sensory only)
  • CN II — Optic: Read the gum wrapper 👁️ — visual acuity (sensory only)
  • CN III, IV, VI: Follow the gum through the H-pattern 👀 — eye movement
  • CN V — Trigeminal: Chew the gum 😬 — facial sensation + chewing (LARGEST CN)
  • CN VII — Facial: Smile, raise eyebrows, puff cheeks — facial expression. Bell's palsy = CN VII!
  • CN VIII — Vestibulocochlear: Hear the wrapper crinkle 👂 — hearing + balance
  • CN IX + X: Swallow, say "Ahh" — gag reflex. No gag = aspiration risk!
  • CN XI — Accessory: Shrug shoulders against resistance 💪
  • CN XII — Hypoglossal: Stick out tongue — deviates TOWARD the damaged side

💥 PACs and PVCs — Premature Beats

⚡A premature beat is an early beat that fires before the SA node's next scheduled beat. The origin tells you everything — did it start in the atria (PAC) or the ventricles (PVC)?
FeaturePAC (Premature Atrial Complex)PVC (Premature Ventricular Complex)
OriginAtria — somewhere other than SA nodeVentricles — below the AV node
P wavePresent but early and abnormal shapeAbsent — no P before the wide beat
QRSNormal (≤0.10 sec) — conduction pathway normalWide (>0.12 sec), bizarre — conduction abnormal
Pause after beatUsually no full compensatory pauseCompensatory pause (large gap after beat)
Patient experienceOften none, possible palpitations"Skipped beat" feeling, stronger next beat
Clinical concernUsually benign — monitorBenign if isolated; concerning if frequent (>6/min), multifocal, R-on-T phenomenon
Memory tipPAC = P wave present → Atria problemPVC = Wide QRS, no P → Ventricle problem
PVC danger signs: Frequency >6/min · Multifocal (different shapes = multiple ectopic foci) · Couplets (2 in a row) · R-on-T phenomenon (PVC landing on T wave = VFib risk) · In the context of acute MI or electrolyte imbalance

❤️ Heart Sounds — Full Detail

🩺Heart sounds are caused by valve closure, not valve opening. Think about it like a door slamming — opening is silent, slamming shut makes the sound.

Normal Heart Sounds

  • S1 ("lub") = Mitral + tricuspid AV valves CLOSE → marks START of systole. Loudest at APEX (5th ICS MCL). S1 > S2 at apex.
  • S2 ("dub") = Aortic + pulmonic semilunar valves CLOSE → marks END of systole. Loudest at BASE (2nd ICS). S2 > S1 at base.
  • S1 = S2 at Erb's point (3rd ICS LSB)

Abnormal Heart Sounds

S3 ("Ken-tuck-Y") — Early diastole, right after S2.
Ventricles filling rapidly with too much volume.
Adults >40 = PATHOLOGICAL → Heart failure
S1-S2-S3 sounds like "Kentucky"
S4 ("Ten-nes-SEE") — Late diastole, just before S1.
Atria squeezing against a stiff, non-compliant ventricle.
ALWAYS pathological → hypertrophy, HTN, CAD
S4-S1-S2 sounds like "Tennessee"

Auscultation Landmarks

"All People Enjoy Time Magazine"
Aortic = 2nd ICS Right Sternal Border
Pulmonic = 2nd ICS Left Sternal Border
Erb's = 3rd ICS Left Sternal Border (S1=S2)
Tricuspid = 4th ICS Left Sternal Border
Mitral = 5th ICS Midclavicular Line (APEX)

Heart Murmurs

  • Caused by turbulent blood flow through a valve
  • Stenosis = Valve opening is too narrow → turbulence going THROUGH the valve
  • Regurgitation/Insufficiency = Valve cannot close properly → turbulence BACKFLOWING
  • Systolic murmur at 2nd ICS RSB + symptoms → Aortic stenosis until proven otherwise
  • Murmurs graded I–VI (I = barely audible, VI = heard without stethoscope)

📍 12-Lead ECG Electrode Placement

🔌Accurate electrode placement is essential — wrong placement = wrong reading = wrong diagnosis. Each lead views the heart from a different angle.

Precordial (Chest) Leads

  • V1 = 4th ICS Right Sternal Border
  • V2 = 4th ICS Left Sternal Border
  • V3 = Between V2 and V4
  • V4 = 5th ICS Left Midclavicular Line
  • V5 = 5th ICS Left Anterior Axillary Line
  • V6 = 5th ICS Left Midaxillary Line

Limb Leads + Territory

  • 4 limb leads (RA, LA, RL, LL)
  • Inferior wall (RCA) = Leads II, III, aVF
  • Lateral wall (LCx) = Leads I, aVL, V5, V6
  • Anterior wall (LAD) = Leads V1–V4
  • Septal (LAD) = V1, V2
Inferior STEMI (II, III, aVF) → check right-sided leads (V4R) for RV involvement → if positive, avoid nitrates!

🦵 Peripheral Vascular Assessment

🩸The peripheral vascular system brings blood out to the extremities (arteries) and returns it to the heart (veins). Assessment involves checking pulses, skin, temperature, capillary refill, and edema. Problems can be arterial (not enough blood getting out) or venous (blood not getting back).

Key Arteries to Assess

  • Temporal · Carotid · Brachial · Radial · Ulnar
  • Femoral · Popliteal · Dorsalis pedis · Posterior tibial
  • Pulse grading: 0=absent, 1+=weak/thready, 2+=normal, 3+=bounding

5 P's of Peripheral Vascular Assessment

  • Pain — Location, intensity (0–10), quality, changes with movement
  • Pallor — Compare affected limb to unaffected; pale, cyanotic, or mottled = poor perfusion
  • Pulses — Palpate distal pulses, grade 0–3+
  • Paresthesia — Numbness, tingling, burning = nerve/vascular compromise
  • Paralysis — Active ROM, muscle strength, movement against resistance
The 5 P's together = compartment syndrome assessment. All 5 abnormal = emergency. Report immediately.

Key Veins to Assess

  • Jugular veins (JVD = elevated CVP → right heart failure)
  • Deep veins: Femoral, popliteal (DVT risk)
  • Superficial: Great saphenous, small saphenous

DVT Assessment

  • Unilateral leg swelling, redness, warmth, pain
  • Homan's sign (calf pain with dorsiflexion) — unreliable, rarely used now
  • Confirm with Doppler ultrasound
  • Risk: Virchow's triad = stasis + hypercoagulability + endothelial injury
  • Complication: pulmonary embolism (sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, ↓SpO2)

Orthostatic Hypotension

  • BP drops ≥20 mmHg systolic OR ≥10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing
  • Causes: dehydration, medications (antihypertensives, diuretics), prolonged bed rest
  • Assess: lying → sitting → standing, wait 1 minute at each position
  • Nursing: fall precautions, dangle legs before standing, hydration

🫁 COPD & Respiratory Assessment — Full Detail

COPD — The Why Behind O2 Rules

Normal breathing drive: High CO2 → brain tells lungs to breathe faster (hypercapnic drive).

COPD with chronic CO2 retention: The brain has gotten used to high CO2 and no longer responds to it. The ONLY remaining drive to breathe is LOW OXYGEN (hypoxic drive).

Give high-flow O2 → raises SpO2 → removes the hypoxic drive → patient STOPS breathing → CO2 builds → CO2 narcosis → respiratory failure.
  • Target SpO2: 88–92% — not the usual 95%+
  • Best device: Venturi mask (most precise FiO2 delivery)
  • Never give a non-rebreather mask to a chronic COPD patient

ABG Interpretation — Step by Step

Normal values:
pH: 7.35–7.45 | PaCO2: 35–45 | HCO3: 22–26 | PaO2: 80–100
ROME Method:
Respiratory = Opposite: pH↑ CO2↓ or pH↓ CO2↑
Metabolic = Equal: pH↑ HCO3↑ or pH↓ HCO3↓
Four patterns:
pH↓ + CO2↑ = Respiratory ACIDOSIS (hypoventilation, COPD, opioids)
pH↑ + CO2↓ = Respiratory ALKALOSIS (hyperventilation, anxiety, pain)
pH↓ + HCO3↓ = Metabolic ACIDOSIS (DKA, renal failure, lactic acidosis)
pH↑ + HCO3↑ = Metabolic ALKALOSIS (vomiting, loop diuretics)
Compensated ABG: If the "non-primary" value has shifted in the same direction as the pH change, the body is compensating. Full compensation = pH returns to normal. Partial = trending toward normal but not there yet. Example: pH 7.32, CO2 60, HCO3 30 = Respiratory acidosis with partial metabolic compensation (kidneys retaining bicarb to buffer the acidosis).

💉 Module 3 — Peripheral IV Therapy & Venous Access

🔧Think of IV therapy like a water pipe going straight into the body. Instead of swallowing medicine and waiting for it to travel through the stomach, IV medicine goes DIRECTLY into the blood. That means it works super fast — but it also means if something goes wrong, it happens fast too. That's why nurses check the site ALL THE TIME.

🩹 IV Catheter Gauges — Know These Cold

Smaller number = BIGGER hole = FASTER flow
  • 14 (Orange/Magma) = Trauma, massive hemorrhage
  • 16 (Gray) = Major surgery, large volume, hemodynamically unstable
  • 18 (Green) = Large volume infusions, rapid/multiple transfusions
  • 20 (Pink) = Medications, hydration, transfusions (minimum for blood)
  • 22 (Blue) = Small veins, short-term access. Usually can't administer blood
  • 24 (Yellow) = Fragile/pediatric veins, last resort for adults

⚠️ IV Complications — Recognize & Act

Phlebitis Scale (0–4)

  • 0 = No symptoms
  • 1+ = Erythema ± pain
  • 2+ = + Edema + pain
  • 3+ = + Streak + palpable cord
  • 4+ = + Cord >1 inch + purulent drainage → notify provider, culture site
All grades: remove catheter, warm compresses, document, restart elsewhere

Infiltration Scale (0–4)

  • 0 = No symptoms
  • 1 = Edema <1 inch, cool, pale
  • 2 = Edema 1–6 inches, cool, pale
  • 3 = Gross edema >6 inches, pain, possible numbness
  • 4 = Pitting edema, skin tight/leaking, severe pain
⭐ ANY vesicant or blood product infiltration = automatically Grade 4

🧪 Vesicants — Know Every Single One

Vesicant = any drug that causes blistering, severe tissue injury, or necrosis if it leaks out of the vein. ANY amount = Grade 4 extravasation = emergency.
  • Catecholamines: Dopamine, Epinephrine, Norepinephrine (Levophed) → antidote: Phentolamine
  • Vinca Alkaloids: Vincristine, Vinblastine (chemo drugs)
  • Others: Gentamycin, Mannitol
Extravasation Treatment (STOP order):
1️⃣ STOP infusion immediately
2️⃣ Keep catheter IN (need it for antidote delivery!)
3️⃣ Notify Pharmacy (antidote order)
4️⃣ Notify Provider
5️⃣ Clean the site
6️⃣ Remove IV AFTER antidote given
7️⃣ Photograph + Document everything

🔢 IV Drip Rate Calculations

Formula: (mL/hr × drop factor) ÷ 60 = gtt/min

Shortcuts:
10 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 6
15 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 4
20 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 3
60 gtt/mL (microdrip) → gtt/min = mL/hr exactly!

🚨 IV Safety Rules — Never Break These

  • 🔴 KCl NEVER IV push — causes immediate cardiac arrest. Always diluted, always pumped, max 10 mEq/hr peripheral
  • 🔴 Vancomycin ≥60 minutes — faster = Red Man Syndrome (flushing, pruritus). Slow down + diphenhydramine. NOT an allergy.
  • 🔴 Blood = 0.9% NS only — never LR (calcium → clots), never D5W (hemolysis)
  • 🔴 Phenytoin = NS only — precipitates in ANY dextrose solution
  • 🔴 Pip-tazo + D5W = incompatible — flush with NS before and after
  • 🔴 Scrub the hub ≥15 seconds before every IV access — prevents CLABSI
  • 🔴 No re-palpating after chlorhexidine prep with ungloved finger — must reclean

📍 IV Site Selection — Full Detail

🔍Choosing the right site is the most important step. A good site = successful insertion, fewer complications, better outcomes. A rushed choice = multiple attempts, patient discomfort, and delayed treatment.

Preferred Sites (start distal, work proximal)

  • Dorsal hand veins — easily visible but smaller; limited for blood products or rapid infusions
  • Forearm veins (cephalic, basilic) — preferred for routine IV access
  • Antecubital fossa (brachial, median cubital) — large veins, great for blood draws or urgent access; BUT arm flexion causes occlusion alarms and kinking
  • Non-dominant hand/arm preferred when possible

Sites to AVOID

  • Arm with history of mastectomy or lymph node dissection
  • Arm with AV fistula (dialysis patient) — absolute contraindication
  • Arm with cast, paralysis, or reduced sensation
  • Site below a previous phlebitis or infiltration
  • Palmar surface of wrist — nerve damage risk
  • Edematous extremities (difficult to assess, increased infection risk)
  • Tattooed areas (difficult to assess complications)

Vein Dilation Techniques

  • Tourniquet — snug but not cutting arterial flow; check radial pulse; release periodically
  • Dependent position — hang arm below heart level, gravity pools blood distally
  • Warm compresses — 3–5 minutes promotes vasodilation
  • Pump fist — pumping hand with arm lower than heart increases venous filling
  • Stroke downward along the vein
  • Friction from cleansing also dilates vein

Two-Attempt Rule

No more than 2 attempts per clinician. After 2 failed attempts → seek higher skill level clinician or vascular access team. Multiple attempts = pain, vein damage, delayed care, lost trust.

Venipuncture Technique

  • Comfortable position · Dilate vein · Cleanse with chlorhexidine in circular motion outward · Allow to dry
  • Stabilize vein by anchoring distal to insertion site
  • Bevel UP at 10–30° angle
  • Watch for flashback (blood in chamber)
  • Advance catheter, withdraw needle, connect tubing

⚠️ Systemic Complications of IV Therapy

Local complications (phlebitis, infiltration, extravasation) affect only the IV site. Systemic complications affect the whole body and can be life-threatening.
ComplicationSigns & SymptomsNursing ActionPrevention
Pulmonary Edema / Fluid Overload ↓SpO2, ↑RR, crackles in lung bases, restlessness, dyspnea, pink frothy sputum, JVD HOB up, apply O2, vital signs, CV assessment, notify provider STAT, anticipate diuretics Monitor I&O, infuse at ordered rate, check lung sounds before and after, caution in elderly and renal/cardiac patients

Catheter-Related Bloodstream Infection (CRBSI) / Sepsis Fever, ↑HR, hypotension, altered mental status, purulent drainage at site Notify provider, blood cultures (before antibiotics), remove IV catheter and culture tip, IV antibiotics per protocol Strict hand hygiene and aseptic technique at insertion and every access. CLABSI vs CRBSI: CLABSI = Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection (central lines). CRBSI = Catheter-Related Bloodstream Infection (any vascular device). Both are hospital-acquired preventable events — they are tracked as adverse outcomes. The nurse is the frontline defense.

👶👴 Lifespan Considerations in IV Therapy

Pediatric Patients

  • Access sites: hand, foot, scalp (neonates — scalp requires special training)
  • Smaller vasculature → 22–24 gauge common
  • Arm boards and gauze wraps improve stability
  • Topical anesthetics (lidocaine cream) minimize insertion pain
  • Use child life specialists, distraction toys, procedural rooms
  • Educate parents on their supportive role BEFORE the procedure
  • Lower extremity access requires provider order (perfusion concerns)

Older Adults

  • Fragile, superficial veins — easier to see but burst more easily
  • Consider omitting tourniquet to prevent vein rupture ("blowing" the vein)
  • Insert at a shallower angle (10°) — less subcutaneous tissue
  • Skin tears possible from tape — use gentler securement
  • Patients on steroids have especially fragile veins — extra caution
  • Monitor more closely for fluid overload (decreased cardiac/renal reserve)

📝 IV Therapy Documentation Requirements

What to document at insertion: Gauge · Site location (e.g., 20G PIV left cephalic vein) · Number of attempts · Skin prep used · Dressing applied · Patient response · Date and time
Ongoing assessment documentation: Site appearance (use phlebitis/infiltration scale) · Dressing integrity (clean, dry, intact) · Patency (flushes without resistance, blood return present) · Infusion type and rate · Any complications and interventions
Site change documentation: Reason for removal (completed therapy, phlebitis grade X, infiltration grade X, patient request) · Site condition on removal · New site established
Six Rights of IV Medication Administration:
Right Patient · Right Drug · Right Dose · Right Route · Right Time · Right Documentation
Plus: Right to Refuse · Right Reason · Right Response (assess before and after)

🧮 IV Calculation — Complete Reference

Master formula: (Volume in mL ÷ Time in hours) × Drop factor ÷ 60 = gtt/min
Or simply: mL/hr × drop factor ÷ 60 = gtt/min

Drop Factor Shortcuts

  • 10 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 6
  • 15 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 4
  • 20 gtt/mL → mL/hr ÷ 3
  • 60 gtt/mL (microdrip) → gtt/min = mL/hr exactly
Example: 1000 mL NS over 8 hours with 15 gtt/mL tubing
1000 ÷ 8 = 125 mL/hr
125 × 15 ÷ 60 = 31 gtt/min
OR: 125 ÷ 4 = 31 gtt/min ✅

15-Second Check

Once you calculate gtt/min, verify by counting drops in the drip chamber for 15 seconds.

15-sec count = gtt/min ÷ 4

For 31 gtt/min → count 8 drops in 15 seconds. Adjust the roller clamp until you hit the target.

Infusion Pump vs. Gravity

  • Pump → program in mL/hr (most common in hospital)
  • Gravity → calculate gtt/min, adjust roller clamp, count drops
  • Pumps have drug libraries — always confirm drug, concentration, and rate with second nurse for high-alert medications
